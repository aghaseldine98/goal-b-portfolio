What-if: a second way of choosing, 2026-11-12

  • The question: would a rule that weighs price momentum and Street estimate revisions alongside value give a better portfolio than the one we have?
  • The short answer: it gives a better-balanced version of the same value portfolio, not an AI-wave portfolio. The checker passed it.
  • How it was tested: two agent runs, each building a book from cash on the same evidence (the 10 November close). One used today's rule, one the new rule. The checker then reviewed the new-rule book. The design was written down and locked before anything was computed (v2b/validation/2026-11-11/lens_whatif).
  • Nothing changes until you decide. Tomorrow's book (cycle_3) executes as planned.

What the new rule changed:

  • Out: Oracle, Intuit and Microsoft. Their prices had been falling (Intuit and Oracle are in the bottom tenth of the candidates on momentum), which is how cheap stocks often stay cheap.
  • In: Marathon Petroleum, Jabil and Franklin Templeton, all cheap and with rising estimates. Sanmina is also larger.
  • The rest is shared: 8 of the 12 holdings are common to all three books.

How much of that is the rule

  • Today's rule, run again from cash, already differs from the current book by about 20%. That's ordinary run-to-run variation plus history.
  • The new rule differs from that rerun by about 35%. So the rule does change the book, but by a moderate amount, and each arm is one run.

Why it still isn't the Claude Portfolio

The new rule ranked memory and AI-hardware names near the top: SanDisk 2nd, Micron 3rd, Western Digital 13th, Lumentum 16th. The agent still declined every one of them:

  • SanDisk and Micron "assume today's record profits last forever".
  • Lumentum and Western Digital are worth well under their prices on the research's forecasts.

The rule allows holding a stock that looks expensive, but the agent still has to answer "why at this price?". For these names it couldn't.

A portfolio that rides the AI wave has to own stocks it believes are overpriced because they're rising. That's a momentum strategy. The research supports momentum, and it also shows that momentum can crash suddenly when the market turns. I wouldn't go that far without your explicit choice. Even this version stops making the current book's bet against chips.

Is the new book defensible?

Yes, by our standards:

  • The checker passed it and found nothing material.
  • It has the same expected return, with lower volatility, a smaller gap to the index, much better momentum and revision scores, and no bet against chips.

The checker's minor findings:

  • It overstated how close Lear is to its sell trigger.
  • It gave a loose basis for one Marathon Petroleum adjustment.
  • It called Nvidia's step-up "structural" when the research says it's cyclical.
  • Our own records disagree on whether an energy company like Marathon Petroleum can be held. That is our labelling problem, and I'll fix it.

If you switch

  • Cost: moving from tomorrow's book to the new-rule book trades 28% of it. The monthly limit is 20%.
  • Cleanest way: run the next cycle (Tuesday 17 November) under the new rule, starting from the actual book. The limit then phases the change in over about two cycles, with no exception needed.
  • My recommendation: switch that way. It's a modest, defensible improvement. If you want the AI-wave version, that's a separate, riskier choice we'd make deliberately.

Launch review: how the portfolio should choose stocks

12 November 2026. Written before any change is made. Every source is saved on disk under facts/2026-11/literature/ (launch_review_2026-11-12 and factor_lenses_2026-11-11); the long-run numbers come from the Ken French data library (CRSP, to September 2026) and the code that computed them is in v2b/validation/2026-11-12/launch_review/.

The question and the answer

Should the agent keep choosing stocks mainly on value (the original rule), move to equal weights of value, price momentum and estimate revisions (the second rule, tested yesterday), or tip the balance further?

Answer: tip it, but towards earnings momentum, not price momentum, and keep value as the balancing leg. Three findings drive that:

1. In large US stocks, which is our whole universe, value is the weakest of the three signals. Momentum works regardless of company size; value fades as companies get bigger (Israel & Moskowitz 2013). Over the last twenty years a long-only large-cap value tilt returned exactly what the market returned, with four times the tracking error of a momentum tilt.

2. The best-evidenced way to hold momentum without its crashes is to measure it in earnings, not prices. Novy-Marx (2015) shows that earnings momentum explains price momentum, and that building the strategy on earnings surprises "eliminates the crashes... without reducing the strategies' high average returns".

3. Earnings momentum is the signal you described: estimates rising and the price responding is a trend worth being in; estimates rising and the price no longer responding is the market telling you it has already paid for the news. The second half is the exit rule the current design lacks.

What the evidence does not support is a pure trend-following book, a factor-timing switch, or any expectation that the lenses themselves will produce much return. In large caps, every published tilt has decayed towards zero since it was published. The lenses are a risk posture and a trap-avoidance device. If this portfolio beats the index over years, it will be because the research picks better businesses within that posture.

1. The objective, pinned down

The mandate as written: a concentrated, long-only, unlevered book of 8 to 15 Russell 1000 stocks, measured against the index (IWB), discretionary turnover under 20% of the book a month, run by the agent, with every decision recorded and audited. A model portfolio.

Your stated aspiration: a system that could have entered a major trend early, when valuations already looked high, and left when the market started shrugging at big earnings beats.

A measurement fact that changes how to decide. The book's tracking error against the index is 11 to 14% a year. The number of years needed before a return record is statistically distinguishable from luck (t-statistic of 2):

Nobody will learn whether this portfolio has skill from its returns in the next several years. A fund in this position launches on the strength of its process, and it chooses the rule it would still believe in after a bad year, because a bad year is a coin flip whatever the rule. That is the standard applied below.

The comparison you had in mind. Finance Magnates (7 September 2026) reports that AI Finance Labs' Claude Portfolio launched in March with $50,000, was up 19.04% against 12.24% for the S&P 500 by 4 August, and showed 31.1% since launch on Autopilot by 4 September. The same article says Claude "did not independently manage the portfolio. It was used for research and investment ideas, while Lopez-Lira and his team set the strategy and risk parameters and controlled the investment process." Over 3 March to 5 September the semiconductor index (SMH) rose 45%, the Russell 1000 13.5%, growth 10.2% and value 16.7%. A human-steered portfolio with a heavy semiconductor weight in that window is consistent with those numbers. That is a reason to define our own edge, not a template to copy, and the professor who built it has published research showing that returns to LLM signals "decline as LLM adoption rises" (Lopez-Lira & Tang).

2. Where the return is supposed to come from

A product needs a stated source of return and evidence for it. There are three candidates.

What this says:

  • The premiums have mostly gone from large caps. McLean & Pontiff (2016) measured a 58% fall in anomaly returns after publication across 97 predictors; the table shows the same. Over twenty years, no large-cap tilt earned more than half a point a year. Nobody should expect the lenses to make money by themselves.
  • The one thing that held in every period is the penalty on large-cap losers: behind the market in 60 to 76% of three-year windows in each sub-period. "Cheap and getting cheaper" is the most reliable pattern in the table. The current book holds Oracle and Intuit at the 7th and 5th momentum percentiles of the candidates.
  • Value is a large-cap weakling. Israel & Moskowitz: "The value premium decreases with firm size and is weak among the largest stocks. In contrast, momentum profits exhibit no reliable relation with size." Value's 3-year drawdown against the market, 40%, is twice momentum's, and its 2007–2020 drawdown as a long-short factor was 58% and has not been recovered. Arnott et al. attribute that drawdown to valuation spreads and to book value missing intangibles; our anchor uses forecast cash flows, not book value, which helps, but the horizon problem (two years of forecast against prices that pay for ten) is ours.
  • The blend is the robust posture. Value and momentum are negatively correlated (−0.41 as factors; −0.26 as long-only active returns), so half-and-half has the smallest tracking error, the shallowest worst periods and the fewest losing windows in every sub-period. That is the finding of Asness, Moskowitz & Pedersen (2013) and it holds in our own numbers.

2b. Security selection by the research

This is the only place a durable edge could live, and the honest position is that it is unproven:

  • The published evidence that language models read filings better than analysts is thin. Lopez-Lira & Tang (2023, revised 2025) find GPT-4 predicts the market reaction to headlines, strongest for small stocks and negative news, with returns declining as adoption rises. Kim, Muhn & Nikolaev (2024) reported GPT-4 beating analysts at predicting the direction of earnings changes, but the arXiv record now states that the paper has been withdrawn by its author, so it cannot be relied on.
  • Our own record (v2b/validation/2026-11-11/research_bias): over 35 companies that reported after their study was sealed, the research's year-one revenue forecasts were as accurate as the Street's (median absolute error 3.4% against 4.1%). Year two sits 11% below the Street for the fastest-growing third, which is the "growth flattens" prior. All twelve studies checked in October took the company's guidance midpoint as given although companies beat it 91% of the time.
  • The pre-registered forecast-skill records (fsk-1, gbr-1) start scoring from August 2027. Until then, selection skill is an assumption, and the design should not lean on it.

2c. Timing

Asness (2016): timing factors on their own valuations is "very weak historically". Daniel & Moskowitz: momentum crashes are partly forecastable (bear markets, high volatility, then a rebound) and a dynamic weighting roughly doubles the strategy's Sharpe ratio. The two are not in conflict, but the second describes a long-short strategy. For a long-only book the crash is mostly the losers' rebound, which we do not short:

Long-only momentum's cost in a rebound is lagging the market by 10 to 20 points for a year, not a crash. Novy-Marx's result is the better answer for us: measure momentum in earnings and the crash behaviour largely disappears, without a timing rule to get wrong.

3. What our system actually does today

  • The decision ranks on the value anchor: the return today's price offers if the study's two-year forecast is right, with growth fading after year two. The agent treats a low anchor as close to a veto. In yesterday's test it scored 92nd percentile on value even under the new rule, and declined SanDisk and Micron, the second- and third-ranked names on the combined score, because their cheapness "assumes today's record profits last forever".
  • The current book's profile among the candidates: value 91st percentile, momentum 37th, revisions 51st. The lens book: 92, 51, 65. Neither is a momentum book.
  • Correcting the research's forecasts barely changes the ranking (Nvidia 11th to 3rd; eight of twelve holdings stay in the top fifteen under every variant, including the Street's own numbers). The value tilt is the rule, not the analyst.
  • Two decision runs on identical evidence have differed by about 20% of the book. Any rule change smaller than that is invisible.
  • The agent's demonstrated strengths are business quality, accounting adjustments the mechanical anchor misses (Oracle's debt, Tenet's minority partners) and plain explanation. Its demonstrated weakness is anything priced beyond a two-year horizon; it cannot see it, so it calls it expensive.

Why not stay with A. It is the posture with the weakest large-cap evidence, the deepest relative drawdowns, and it hard-wires the one reliable penalty in the data. It also cannot do either half of what you asked for.

Why not stop at B. It is a real improvement in balance and it passed verification, but two things remain. Valuation still works as a veto, so the momentum leg is weaker than the weights suggest; and there is no exit signal other than the thesis breaking or a lower anchor appearing.

Why not D. No evidence supports price momentum as a stand-alone rule once earnings news is accounted for (Novy-Marx: "past performance does not have independent power predicting the cross section of expected returns"), it lags hardest in rebounds, and it conflicts with sizing to evidence.

5. The proposed rule (C), and the reasons for each part

1. Four lenses, equal weights, each a percentile among the candidates: value (the anchor's excess over the market), estimate revisions, price reaction to the last results, and price momentum. Equal weights are not tuned; they are the default. Because two of the four are earnings momentum, earnings momentum carries half the score, which is the tilt the evidence supports in large caps.

2. The reaction lens: the stock's return over the session of each results release, less the index, set against the size of the beat. Chan, Jegadeesh & Lakonishok (1996) used exactly this abnormal announcement return as one of three earnings-momentum measures and found it predicts drift after controlling for price momentum. Jegadeesh & Titman found the same pattern around winners' and losers' announcements. We already save every release with its acceptance time and daily prices, so it can be built in code and backfilled.

3. Valuation stops being a veto, inside a cap you set. Asness (1997): momentum "is particularly strong among expensive stocks" and value is "quite weak among winner stocks". The names the current rule refuses are where momentum has worked best. A stock whose anchor is below the market's return may be held when its revisions and reaction are strong and the research supports the business; the reason is recorded as earnings momentum, its expected return is stated honestly, and the total in such positions stays under a limit set by you, not by the agent.

4. The exit rule. A held stock whose estimates keep rising but whose price no longer responds to beats (reaction lens falling through the bottom third of the candidates after a beat), or whose estimates turn down, must be sold or trimmed, or the decision must give a dated reason to keep it. That is the mechanical form of "the market has stopped rewarding the news".

5. No timing guard. Daniel & Moskowitz's guard addresses the short side. For a long-only book, Novy-Marx's earnings-based construction removes most of the crash behaviour, the agent already sizes high-volatility names small, there is no leverage, and the rail caps churn. One fewer rule for the agent to misapply.

6. Value stays in, as a quarter of the score and as the question "what does the price assume?" that every position must still answer. It is the uncorrelated leg, it is what the agent is best at, and the blend's drawdown record is the reason a reviewer would sign this off.

6. What could go wrong with C

  • The lenses add no return. That is the base case from the post-publication record; the gain is balance and trap avoidance.
  • The agent misreads percentiles as evidence. Yesterday's run did, mildly. The verifier catches some of this; the task text must say what a percentile is and is not.
  • Six weeks of revisions is one earnings season, and reaction is one observation per quarter. Both are noisy; that is why neither is the whole score.
  • Entries into a running trend are slowed by the rail; a trend that lasts three months may be half-missed. The rail is yours to amend, not the agent's.
  • A rebound year like 2009 or 2023 would see this book lag the index by 10 to 20 points. So would every other sound rule in one year or another; see section 1.
  • The percentiles rank within our 133 studied candidates, not the market. Expanding coverage improves them.

7. Before launch: what a fund would insist on, and the staged plan

1. Pre-register the C design (lens definitions, the cap, the exit rule, the evaluation metrics) and hash it before computing a single lens value, as yesterday.

2. Build and backfill the reaction lens from the saved releases and prices; publish its definition and coverage.

3. Test C from cash on the same evidence as yesterday's two arms, verify it, and show all three books side by side: holdings, lens profile, sectors, measured factor exposures, expected returns, turnover, the verifier's findings. Two to three agent sessions, about a day of code first.

4. Decide on Tuesday 17 November. My recommendation: run Tuesday under B now. It is tested, it passed, it is strictly better balanced than A, and the rail phases the change in. Move to C the following cycle if its test holds up. Going straight to C untested is what a fund would not do.

5. Evaluate on process, not returns, with metrics fixed in advance and reviewed at six and twelve months: forecast accuracy against the Street (fsk-1, gbr-1); whether the book held bottom-third-momentum names without a dated reason; falsifier outcomes; a quarterly split of the return into index, style (value against growth, semiconductors) and selection; verifier material-finding rate; turnover against the rail; run-to-run variance. The trigger for changing the rule again is a process failure, not a bad quarter.
